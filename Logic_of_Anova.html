Lab 6: Logic of One-Way Analysis of Variance

Reinforcing core conceptual mechanics, variance decomposition, and Type I error control.

1. The Transition Beyond Two Means

In previous labs we compared two sample means using t tests. Now we evaluate scenarios with more than two groups (k > 2).

  • Groups are often referred to as conditions.
  • These conditions represent levels of our independent variable (IV).
  • The outcome being recorded is our dependent variable (DV).
  • k denotes the total number of groups, conditions, or levels.

2. Why Not Run Multiple t Tests?

For a design with k = 3, running pairwise independent sample t tests requires three comparisons:

  1. Level 1 Mean vs Level 2 Mean
  2. Level 1 Mean vs Level 3 Mean
  3. Level 2 Mean vs Level 3 Mean

Doing this inflates our familywise Type I error rate. While individual Type I error is the probability of incorrectly rejecting the null on a single comparison, familywise error reflects the probability of making at least one false positive across the entire collection of tests.

Familywise Type I Error Rate = 1 − (1 − α)Number of t tests

For three tests at α = 0.05:

1 − (1 − 0.05)3 = 1 − (0.95)3 = 1 − 0.8574 = 0.1426

Our real probability of falsely rejecting a null hypothesis jumps from 5% to 14.26%. ANOVA protects against this by conducting a single omnibus test under our predetermined alpha level.

3. Hypotheses and Assumptions

Statistical Hypotheses

  • Null Hypothesis (H0): μ1 = μ2 = μ3. All group means originate from the exact same population, or populations with identical parameters.
  • Alternative Hypothesis (Ha): At least one population mean differs from the others. Not all μk are identical.

Core Assumptions

  1. Normality: Scores within each condition are normally distributed.
  2. Homogeneity of Variances: Each condition shares equal population variance.
  3. Independence of Observations: Every measurement is independent of all others.

Because normality and homogeneity enforce that the shapes and spreads of the groups are identical, the only feature left free to vary is their locations: their means.

4. Partitioning Variance

ANOVA estimates population parameters under the working assumption that the null hypothesis holds true.

Mean Square Error (MSerror or MSwithin)

By homogeneity of variance, all groups share population error variance (σe2). To get an optimal estimate, we calculate the arithmetic mean of all group variances:

MSerror = (∑ sj2) / k

Mean Square Treatment (MStreatment or MSbetween)

By the Central Limit Theorem, the variance of the sampling distribution of means is σM2 = σe2 / n. Rearranging this relationship gives:

MStreatment = sM2 × n

Here sM2 represents the variance computed across the group means themselves, while n is the number of participants per condition.

The F Ratio

F = MStreatment / MSerror = (σe2 + Treatment Effect) / σe2

If no true treatment effect exists, MStreatment estimates only error variance (σe2 + 0), causing F to hover near 1. If an effect exists, MStreatment systematically exceeds MSerror.

6. Laboratory Reflection Questions

Question 1: Visual Inspection Expectation

Prompt: Prior to completing formal arithmetic, inspect the sample means. Do you expect MStreatment to equal MSerror or exceed it? Why?

Guidance: Notice the spread among the sample group means relative to the spread of individual observations within each condition. If group means visibly separate, we anticipate MStreatment to rise above MSerror.

Question 2: Null Assessment

Prompt: After calculating values, does MStreatment match MSerror? What does this outcome indicate regarding the group means?

Guidance: Check the resulting F ratio. When MStreatment exceeds MSerror, sample mean variation is larger than what would be predicted strictly by within group noise alone.
