STEP-BY-STEP HAND CALCULATION GUIDE

Linear Contrasts & Polynomial Trend Analysis

A complete walkthrough of how to build contrast weights, compute contrast statistics by hand, verify orthogonality, and test polynomial trends.

1. Why Do We Need Contrasts After an Omnibus ANOVA?

When you run a one way ANOVA with k ≥ 3 groups, the omnibus F test evaluates one broad null hypothesis:

H0: μ1 = μ2 = μ3 = … = μk

If your omnibus F test is statistically significant, you know that at least one population mean differs from the others, but you do not know which specific groups differ. A linear contrast lets you zoom in and test a specific 1 degree of freedom question (such as comparing Group 1 against Group 2, or comparing a Control group against the average of two Treatment groups).

Why not just run regular two sample t tests?
If Levene's test is non significant (p > .05), we can assume all groups share the same population variance (σ2). Instead of estimating variance from only the two groups being compared, a linear contrast uses MSerror from the entire omnibus ANOVA. This gives you a pooled error estimate based on all N participants and gives your test more degrees of freedom (dferror = N − k).

2. Step-by-Step: How to Build Contrast Weights (cj)

A linear contrast (denoted by ψ, pronounced "psi") is a weighted sum of your group means (Mj). To set up any contrast by hand, follow this 4 step recipe to assign a weight (cj) to every group:

Step 1 Write down all k groups in order and zero out uninvolved groups

Every group in your ANOVA must receive a weight. If a group is not part of your specific research question, assign it a weight of 0. Multiplying its mean by 0 removes it from the comparison while keeping its variance inside MSerror.

Step 2 Divide the remaining groups into Side A (+) and Side B (−)

A contrast always pits one set of groups against another set of groups. Give all groups on Side A positive weights (+) and all groups on Side B negative weights (−). Which side gets + or − only flips the sign of ψ and t; the F statistic and p value stay identical.

Step 3 Balance the weights so Side A and Side B sum to the same magnitude

If you are comparing 1 group vs. 1 group, use +1 and −1.
If you are comparing 1 group vs. the average of 2 groups, you can either use fractions (+1 vs. −1/2, −1/2) OR whole numbers (+2 vs. −1, −1). Both options produce the exact same t and F values!

Step 4 Verify the Sum Rule: ∑cj = 0

Add all of your weights across all k groups. They must add up to zero: c1 + c2 + … + ck = 0. If they do not sum to zero, it is not a valid contrast.

Reference Table: Common Contrast Weight Patterns (for k = 3 Groups)

Question Being Asked Group 1 (c1) Group 2 (c2) Group 3 (c3) Check: ∑cj Algebraic Meaning of ψ
Group 1 vs. Group 2 (ignore Group 3) +1 −1 0 1 − 1 + 0 = 0 M1 − M2
Group 2 vs. Group 3 (ignore Group 1) 0 +1 −1 0 + 1 − 1 = 0 M2 − M3
Group 1 vs. Average of Groups 2 & 3 (Fractions) +1 −1/2 −1/2 1 − 0.5 − 0.5 = 0 M1 − 0.5(M2 + M3)
Group 1 vs. Average of Groups 2 & 3 (Integers) +2 −1 −1 2 − 1 − 1 = 0 2M1 − M2 − M3

3. Step-by-Step: Computing a Contrast by Hand

Once you have your weights (cj), the group means (Mj), the group sample sizes (nj), and MSerror from the omnibus ANOVA table, follow these 5 steps to calculate the test statistic by hand.

Step 1 Calculate the Contrast Value (ψ)

Multiply each group's contrast weight (cj) by its group mean (Mj), then add those products together:

ψ = ∑(cjMj) = (c1 × M1) + (c2 × M2) + … + (ck × Mk)
Step 2 Square Each Weight and Sum Them (∑cj2)

Square every group's weight (remember that squaring a negative number makes it positive!) and add the squared weights together:

∑(cj2) = c12 + c22 + … + ck2
Step 3 Compute the Standard Error of the Contrast (SEψ) and the t-Statistic

If all groups have the same sample size (n), plug MSerror, n, and ∑(cj2) into the Standard Error formula, then divide ψ by SEψ:

SEψ = √[ (MSerror / n) × ∑(cj2) ]    ⇒    t = ψ / SEψ   (with df = N − k)

(If group sample sizes are unequal, compute SEψ = √[ MSerror × ∑(cj2 / nj) ].)

Step 4 Compute SScontrast and Fcontrast (Alternative ANOVA Path)

You can also compute the Sum of Squares for the contrast directly and divide by MSerror to get the F ratio. Because every single contrast has dfcontrast = 1, MScontrast is equal to SScontrast:

SScontrast = ( n × ψ2 ) / ∑(cj2)    ⇒    Fcontrast = SScontrast / MSerror
Step 5 Cross-Check Your Math Using F = t2

Any 1 degree of freedom F test (including a two group ANOVA or a single linear contrast) is mathematically identical to a squared t test: F = t2 (and |t| = √F). Square your t from Step 3 to verify that it equals your F from Step 4!

Worked Example: Taste Study (3 Groups, n = 5 per Group)

Suppose 15 participants (n = 5 per group) rate three types of cookies on a 1 to 8 scale:
Group 1 (Vanilla): M1 = 3.20  |  Group 2 (Chocolate): M2 = 6.40  |  Group 3 (Peanut Butter): M3 = 6.00.
From the omnibus ANOVA, MSerror = 1.50 with dferror = 15 − 3 = 12.
Research Question: Does Vanilla (Group 1) differ from the average of the two nut/cocoa cookies (Groups 2 & 3)?

  1. Choose Weights: Give Vanilla c1 = −2, Chocolate c2 = +1, and Peanut Butter c3 = +1. (Check: −2 + 1 + 1 = 0).
  2. Step 1 (Find ψ): ψ = (−2)(3.20) + (+1)(6.40) + (+1)(6.00) = −6.40 + 6.40 + 6.00 = 6.00.
  3. Step 2 (Find ∑cj2): ∑cj2 = (−2)2 + (+1)2 + (+1)2 = 4 + 1 + 1 = 6.
  4. Step 3 (Find SEψ and t):
    SEψ = √[ (1.50 / 5) × 6 ] = √[ 0.30 × 6 ] = √1.80 = 1.342.
    t = 6.00 / 1.342 = 4.472.
  5. Step 4 (Find SScontrast and F):
    SScontrast = (5 × 6.002) / 6 = (5 × 36) / 6 = 30.00.
    Fcontrast = 30.00 / 1.50 = 20.00.
  6. Step 5 (Verify F = t2): t2 = (4.472)2 = 20.00. The calculations match!

4. Step-by-Step: Testing Orthogonality & Applying Bonferroni

When you run more than one contrast on the same dataset, you need to know two things: (1) Are the contrasts testing completely independent pieces of variance (orthogonality)? (2) How do we protect against Type I error inflation across multiple tests (Bonferroni correction)?

Part A: 3-Step Recipe to Test if Two Contrasts are Orthogonal

Two contrasts are orthogonal if the sum of the products of their corresponding weights equals zero (∑(c1j × c2j) = 0 for equal sample sizes). Here is how to show your work in a table:

Step 1 Verify Each Contrast Individually Sums to Zero

Add the weights across Row 1 (∑c1j = 0) and across Row 2 (∑c2j = 0) to make sure both are valid contrasts.

Step 2 Multiply Vertical Pairs (Column by Column)

Create a third row in your table called Product (c1j × c2j). In each group's column, multiply the Contrast 1 weight by the Contrast 2 weight.

Step 3 Add the Products Across the Bottom Row

Sum the products across all k columns: ∑(c1j × c2j).
• If the sum equals 0, the contrasts are orthogonal.
• If the sum is any number other than 0, the contrasts are NOT orthogonal.

Worked Example: Checking Orthogonality with Fractional Weights

Let's test whether Contrast 1 (0, +1, −1) and Contrast 2 (+1, −1/2, −1/2) are orthogonal:

Row Description Group 1 Group 2 Group 3 Row Sum
Contrast 1 (c1j) 0 +1 −1 0 + 1 − 1 = 0 (Valid)
Contrast 2 (c2j) +1 −1/2 −1/2 1 − 1/2 − 1/2 = 0 (Valid)
Product (c1j × c2j) (0)(+1) = 0 (+1)(−1/2) = −1/2 (−1)(−1/2) = +1/2 0 + (−1/2) + (+1/2) = 0

Conclusion: Because both rows sum to 0 and the sum of the cross products (∑c1jc2j) equals 0, these two contrasts are orthogonal.

Part B: How to Apply the Bonferroni Correction by Hand (and in R)

When you run m comparisons, your Familywise Type I Error Rate increases. The Bonferroni correction adjusts for conducting m tests:

  • By Hand (Adjusting Alpha): Divide your starting alpha (α = 0.05) by the number of contrasts you ran (m): αBonferroni = 0.05 / m. For example, if you run m = 2 contrasts, a contrast is only significant if its unadjusted p value is less than 0.05 / 2 = 0.025. If you run m = 3 pairwise contrasts, each needs p < 0.05 / 3 = 0.0167.
  • In Software like R (`adjust = "bonferroni"`): Instead of shrinking α, R multiplies each contrast's raw p value by m: pbonf = praw × m (capped at 1.00). You then compare pbonf directly against 0.05. Both methods always give the exact same significance decision!

5. Step-by-Step: Polynomial Trend Analysis

What if your independent variable is not nominal categories (like cookie flavors), but quantitative, ordered levels (such as Study Time: 1 min, 2 min, 3 min, 4 min)? Instead of just asking whether 1 minute differs from 2 minutes, we want to know the overall shape (trend) across all levels.

Polynomial trend analysis is simply a set of pre built orthogonal contrasts designed to detect specific geometric shapes across ordered group means:

  • Linear Trend: Do the means consistently rise or fall along a straight line (0 bends)?
  • Quadratic Trend: Does the pattern curve or level off with 1 bend (a U shape, inverted U, or diminishing returns plateau)?
  • Cubic Trend: Does the pattern reverse direction twice with 2 bends (an S shaped wave)?
The (k − 1) Rule for Trends: With k ordered groups, you can test up to k − 1 orthogonal polynomial trends. For k = 4 groups, dfbetween = 3, which splits cleanly into Linear (1 df) + Quadratic (1 df) + Cubic (1 df). Because these three polynomial contrasts are mutually orthogonal, their Sums of Squares add up to the entire omnibus SSbetween:
SSbetween = SSlinear + SSquadratic + SScubic

Complete Walkthrough: Study Time & Word Recall (k = 4 Groups, n = 6 per Group)

A researcher assigns N = 24 participants (n = 6 per group) to study a list of words for 1 min, 2 min, 3 min, or 4 min. From the omnibus ANOVA, SSbetween = 172.50 and MSerror = 2.60 (dferror = 20).

Group Level 1 min (Group 1) 2 min (Group 2) 3 min (Group 3) 4 min (Group 4)
Sample Size (nj) 6 6 6 6
Group Mean (Mj) 2.00 6.00 8.00 9.00

Notice the pattern in the group means: from 1 min to 2 min, recall jumps by +4 words (2 to 6); from 2 min to 3 min, it rises by +2 words (6 to 8); from 3 min to 4 min, it rises by +1 word (8 to 9). Let's test the Linear and Quadratic trends step by step using the exact same contrast formulas from Section 3!

1. Step-by-Step Linear Trend (−3, −1, +1, +3)

  • Step 1 (Compute ψlinear): Multiply each Linear weight by its group mean:
    ψlinear = (−3)(2.00) + (−1)(6.00) + (+1)(8.00) + (+3)(9.00) = −6 − 6 + 8 + 27 = 23.00
  • Step 2 (Find ∑cj2): (−3)2 + (−1)2 + (+1)2 + (+3)2 = 9 + 1 + 1 + 9 = 20
  • Step 3 (Compute SSlinear): SSlinear = (n × ψlinear2) / ∑cj2 = (6 × 23.002) / 20 = (6 × 529) / 20 = 158.70
  • Step 4 (Compute Flinear and tlinear):
    Flinear = SSlinear / MSerror = 158.70 / 2.60 = 61.04  (with t = √61.04 = 7.81, p < .001).
    Meaning: Over 92% of the between group variance (158.70 / 172.50) is explained by a straight line increase!

2. Step-by-Step Quadratic Trend (+1, −1, −1, +1)

  • Step 1 (Compute ψquad): Multiply each Quadratic weight by its group mean:
    ψquad = (+1)(2.00) + (−1)(6.00) + (−1)(8.00) + (+1)(9.00) = 2 − 6 − 8 + 9 = −3.00
  • Step 2 (Find ∑cj2): (+1)2 + (−1)2 + (−1)2 + (+1)2 = 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 4
  • Step 3 (Compute SSquad): SSquad = (6 × (−3.00)2) / 4 = (6 × 9) / 4 = 13.50
  • Step 4 (Compute Fquad and tquad):
    Fquad = SSquad / MSerror = 13.50 / 2.60 = 5.19  (with t = −√5.19 = −2.28, p = .034).
    Meaning: There is also a statistically significant 1 bend curve (p < .05), capturing the leveling off (diminishing returns) at higher study times.
