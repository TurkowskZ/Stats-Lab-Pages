Core Comparison of the 3 Statistical Tests

Single Group

One Sample t Test

Comparison against an established baseline

Knowns: Population mean (μ), Sample mean (X̄), Sample SD (s)
Unknown: Population SD (σ)
Research Focus: Is my sample mean significantly different than the established population?
Null Hypothesis: X̄ = μ
Sampling Distributions: One distribution, using sample SD to estimate standard error.
Paired Observations

Dependent Samples t Test

Scores come from the same person

Knowns: Sample mean (X̄), Sample SD (s)
Unknowns: Population mean (μ), Population SD (σ)
Research Focus: Does my sample mean change after my intervention?
Null Hypothesis: X̄₁ = X̄₂
Sampling Distributions: One sampling distribution of differences, estimating standard error with difference SD.
Two Distinct Groups

Independent Samples t Test

Scores come from different people

Knowns: Sample means (X̄₁, X̄₂), Sample SDs (s₁, s₂)
Unknowns: Population mean (μ), Population SD (σ)
Research Focus: Are two samples distinct, suggesting origin from two distinct populations?
Null Hypothesis: μ₁ = μ₂
Distributions: Three total. Two separate sampling distributions plus one difference score distribution.

Critical Assumption Analysis

Homogeneity of Variance & The Welch Satterthwaite Solution

The independent samples t test assumes that the populations from which samples are gathered possess equal variances. Evaluating this condition ensures your test preserves its nominal false positive rate.

Assumption Met: p > .05

The variances of the two samples do not differ significantly. You retain the null hypothesis of equal variances and can comfortably compute standard pooled variance and traditional degrees of freedom (n₁ + n₂ minus 2).

Assumption Violated: p ≤ .05

Heterogeneity of variance is present. Whether this distorts your conclusion depends strictly on whether your group sample sizes are balanced or unbalanced.

How Sample Sizes Dictate The Impact of Heterogeneity
⚖️ Condition One: Violated Assumption With Equal Sample Sizes (n₁ = n₂)

The t distribution is robust to this violation. The unequal variances will not alter the calculated test statistic or conclusion by any meaningful amount. Results obtained with or without correction remain essentially identical.

⚠️ Condition Two: Violated Assumption With Unequal Sample Sizes (n₁ ≠ n₂)

You must employ the Welch Satterthwaite solution for adjusted t prime (t') and adjusted degrees of freedom (df'). If uncorrected, severe distortion occurs:

Smallest Group Has Largest Variance
The observed t statistic becomes inflated. This dramatically escalates Type I error risk (falsely declaring a difference when none truly exists).
Largest Group Has Largest Variance
The observed t statistic becomes underestimated. This escalates Type II error risk (missing a genuine real world effect).
Sample Size Rule of Thumb: These distorting effects are heightened when total sample sizes are small. However, they do not simply vanish automatically in larger samples when severe imbalance exists. Maintaining equal group sizes during experiment design is the ideal safeguard.

Welch Satterthwaite Analytical Formulas

Adjusted Test Statistic (t')
t' = (X̄₁ − X̄₂) √[ (s₁² / n₁) + (s₂² / n₂) ]

Separate variances are used directly without assuming a single pooled standard deviation.

Adjusted Degrees of Freedom (df')
df' = [ (s₁² / n₁) + (s₂² / n₂) ]² [ (s₁² / n₁)² / (n₁ − 1) ] + [ (s₂² / n₂)² / (n₂ − 1) ]

Degrees of freedom become a real numbered fraction, lowering power appropriately to guard against false positives.

Theoretical Principles

Central Limit Theorem & Decision Logic

Every t test relies upon foundational probabilistic principles that connect collected sample data with theoretical sampling distributions.

1

Central Limit Theorem

Allows you to determine the nature of a sampling distribution, which makes formal testing mathematically viable even when population shapes are unknown.

2

Sampling Distribution

Use the variance of your sample or samples to determine the shape, width, and standard error of the relevant sampling distribution.

3

Alpha & Cutoffs

Apply your predefined alpha level to the theoretical distribution to pinpoint the exact critical threshold value required for statistical significance.

4

Rejection Rule

Compare your observed test statistic or p value against the critical boundaries to reach a rigorous rejection or non rejection decision.

Universal Decision Rules for All t Tests
Statistic Comparison Rule
If |t_observed| > t_critical, reject H₀

When your sample deviation exceeds the expected cutoff threshold defined by random chance at the specified alpha level, reject the null hypothesis.

Probability Value (p value) Rule
If p value < α, reject H₀

If the probability of observing a difference this extreme under a true null hypothesis is smaller than alpha, reject the null hypothesis.

Understanding Sampling Distributions in Each Test

One Sample

One Sampling Distribution

Applies the standard deviation of your single sample to directly estimate the standard error of the mean.

Dependent Samples

One Difference Distribution

Calculates difference scores for each paired individual, creating a single distribution of difference scores to estimate standard error.

Independent Samples

Three Sampling Distributions

One for each sample group estimating its own SE, plus a third distribution representing the difference between sample means governed by the Variance Sum Law.
