Executive Companion

Overview of the Three Different t Tests

A side by side structural comparison identifying known parameters, unknown parameters, hypotheses, and distribution properties.

Single Sample

One Sample t Test

Evaluating against a hypothesized population parameter

Knowns: Hypothesized population mean (μ), Sample mean (M), Sample SD (S)
Unknown: Population SD (σ)
Research Question: Does the population from which the sample came differ from the hypothesized parameter?
Null Hypothesis (H₀): μ = μ₀  (parameters, not statistics)
Sampling Distribution: One distribution, using sample standard deviation (S) to estimate standard error (SM = S / √n).
Paired Measures

Dependent Samples t Test

Scores originate from the exact same person

Knowns: Difference scores, Mean of differences (M), Estimated variance (S²)
Unknowns: Population mean difference (μD), Population variance (σ²)
Research Question: Is there a systematic difference between paired scores across conditions?
Null Hypothesis (H₀): μD = 0  (or μ₁ − μ₂ = 0)
Sampling Distribution: One distribution of means of difference scores, using SM = S / √n with df = n − 1.
Two Distinct Groups

Independent Samples t Test

Scores originate from completely different people

Knowns: Sample means (M₁, M₂), Sample variances (S₁², S₂²)
Unknowns: Population means (μ₁, μ₂), Population variances (σ₁², σ₂²)
Research Question: Is there a significant difference in the population means between the two independent groups?
Null Hypothesis (H₀): μ₁ = μ₂  (or μ₁ − μ₂ = 0)
Sampling Distributions: Distribution of differences between means, using pooled variance SPooled² and standard error SDifference.
Key Disciplinary Distinction

Dependent Samples: The two sets of scores being compared come from the same person (pre versus post or matched pairs).

Independent Samples: The two sets of scores being compared come from different people (separate group entities).

Universal Rejection Criteria

Test Statistic Comparison
If |t_observed| > t_critical, reject H₀

The observed difference between sample and baseline exceeds the maximum deviation expected from random sampling error alone.

Probability Value Comparison
If p < α, reject H₀

The probability of obtaining a sample result this extreme assuming the null hypothesis is true falls below your accepted risk threshold.

Sampling Distribution Properties

One Sample t Test

Uses one sampling distribution of means. The unbiased sample standard deviation estimates the standard error of the mean (SM = S / √n).

Dependent Samples t Test

Uses a sampling distribution of means of difference scores. Differences (X₂ − X₁) yield sample variance S² = SS / df, providing SM = S / √n with df = n − 1.

Independent Samples t Test

Uses the sampling distribution of the difference between two means. Standard error of the difference is SDifference = √(SM₁² + SM₂²).

When variances are assumed equal, SM₁² and SM₂² are calculated using the pooled variance estimate SPooled² with dfTotal = df₁ + df₂.

Homogeneity of Variance Assumption

Evaluating equal population variances in independent designs, diagnostic interpretations of Levene test, and corrective protocols.

Assumption Met: p > .05

Variances of the two groups do not differ significantly. Homogeneity of variance holds. Proceed with standard pooled variance and conventional degrees of freedom (dfTotal = df₁ + df₂).

Assumption Violated: p ≤ .05

Heterogeneity of variance is present. Whether this violation invalidates your test conclusions depends directly on sample size balance between your two groups.

How Group Sample Sizes Dictate Violation Impact
⚖️ Condition One: Violated Assumption With Equal Sample Sizes (n₁ = n₂)

The Student t distribution is robust to this violation. When sample sizes are balanced, unequal variances will not affect test results to a meaningful degree. Results computed with or without correction remain essentially identical.

⚠️ Condition Two: Violated Assumption With Unequal Sample Sizes (n₁ ≠ n₂)

You must apply the Welch Satterthwaite solution for adjusted t prime (t') and adjusted degrees of freedom (df'). If uncorrected, systematic distortion damages nominal statistical validity:

Smallest Group Has Largest Variance

The observed t statistic becomes inflated. This elevates Type I error risk (falsely rejecting the null hypothesis and declaring a nonexistent difference).

Largest Group Has Largest Variance

The observed t statistic becomes underestimated. This elevates Type II error risk (failing to detect a genuine experimental difference).

Sample Size Rule of Thumb:

These distortion effects are heightened when total sample sizes are small. However, they will not necessarily improve simply by gathering larger overall samples if severe imbalance persists. Keeping group sample sizes equal in experimental design is your most reliable defense.

Welch Satterthwaite Analytical Solution

Analytical corrections adjusting the denominator and degrees of freedom

Adjusted Test Statistic (t')
t' = (M₁ − M₂) √[ (S₁² / n₁) + (S₂² / n₂) ]

Replaces the pooled variance estimate (SPooled²) with unpooled individual sample variances (S₁², S₂²), correcting denominator scaling.

Adjusted Degrees of Freedom (df')
df' = [ (S₁² / n₁) + (S₂² / n₂) ]² [ (S₁² / n₁)² / (n₁ − 1) ] + [ (S₂² / n₂)² / (n₂ − 1) ]

Adjusts degrees of freedom to a fractional cutoff, elevating critical t thresholds to protect nominal error rates.
