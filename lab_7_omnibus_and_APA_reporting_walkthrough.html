Study Context & Raw Sample Data ($X$)

Researchers examined the impact of caffeine consumption on sustained attention performance accuracy. Participants were randomly sampled and assigned to one of $k = 3$ experimental caffeine conditions: No Caffeine, Low Caffeine, and High Caffeine. Performance was measured via the total number of correct responses ($X$) on a continuous sustained attention battery, where each individual score follows the structural model $X_{ij} = \mu + (\mu_j - \mu) + (X_{ij} - \mu_j) = \mu + \tau_j + \epsilon_{ij}$.

Null Hypothesis

$H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2 = \mu_3 \quad (\mu_A = \mu_B = \mu_C = \dots = \mu_k)$

All $k$ population group means are equal in sustained attention accuracy.

Alternative Hypothesis

$H_1 \text{ (or } H_a\text{)}: \text{The } k \text{ population means are not equal}$

There is at least one mean difference among the groups.

Omnibus ANOVA Source Table (Equal Sample Size)

Effect (Between)
$$SS_{Effect} = \sum^{k} n_i(M_i - M_{\text{Grand}})^2$$
$df_{Between} = k - 1$
Error (Within)
$$SS_{Error} = \sum^{all\ scores} (X - M)^2$$
$df_{Within} = n - k$
Total Variance
$$SS_{Total} = \sum^{scores} (X - M_{\text{Grand}})^2$$
$df_{Total} = n - 1$
$F$ Ratio & Effect Size
$$F = \frac{MS_{Effect}}{MS_{Error}} = \frac{MS_{Between}}{MS_{Within}}$$
$\eta^2 = \dfrac{SS_{between}}{SS_{total}}$

Interpretation of Omnibus Results

Because our computed $F(2, 24) = 6.16$ ($p = .007$) is more extreme than $F_{critical}(2, 24) = 3.40$ at $\alpha = .05$, we reject the null hypothesis (that all the means are equal) and accept our alternative hypothesis (that there is at least one mean difference among the groups).

The Core Challenge: The significant $F$ only indicates that at least two means are significantly different from one another, but you cannot know which specific mean pairs significantly differ until you conduct a planned or post hoc comparison:

  • Which specific group means ($M_1, M_2, M_3$) are significantly different from each other.
  • The direction of the differences (which specific dosage level produces superior attention performance).

Error Rates & Type I Error Inflation

When performing multiple $t$ tests to compare means, the major problem is the inflation of Type I error. Assuming all tests are independent and each is conducted at $\alpha = .05$, the probability that at least one test is a false positive is greater than $\alpha$:

Individual Type I Error Rate

Also termed the error rate per comparison. Generally, when you have one $t$ test, the probability of rejecting a true null hypothesis is set at $\alpha$:

$\text{Type 1 error} = 1 - (.95) = 0.05 \quad (\alpha = .05)$

Family Wise Type I Error Rate

The probability across a family of tests ($c =$ number of $t$ tests) that at least one test is a false positive when all null hypotheses are true:

$\text{Family wise type I error} = 1 - (1 - \alpha)^c$

Comparison Techniques & Decision Framework

A Priori (Planned Contrasts)

Differences between group means ($M_i$) are predicted by prior theory before viewing sample data. Because empirical theory motivates the tests, they retain higher statistical power and usually permit less stringent corrections.

Post Hoc (Unplanned Comparisons)

Follow up examinations conducted after viewing empirical findings, exploring unexpected differences. Because tests are atheoretical and numerous, familywise Type I error inflation must be strictly controlled.

Technique When to Use Stringency Level
Linear Contrasts Specific planned hypotheses testing one group or a set of groups against another. Can be orthogonal (independent) or non orthogonal. Flexible. Options include testing at a stricter alpha, applying Bonferroni adjustments, or conducting fewer total contrasts.
Bonferroni Adjustment When performing a small number of planned comparisons to maintain exact familywise error limits ($\alpha / c$). Less stringent with very few comparisons, but becomes increasingly stringent as comparisons grow larger.
Tukey HSD When testing all possible pairwise combinations among group means ($M_i$ vs $M_j$), either planned a priori or exploratory post hoc. Moderately stringent. Maintains excellent power balance compared to Scheffe while controlling familywise alpha.
Scheffe Method Post hoc complex linear combinations and exploratory data mining across many treatment conditions. Most stringent method available. Greatly reduces Type I error but increases the probability of Type II error.

Methodological Perspectives on Corrections

Standard Textbook Recommendation:

Argues that whether comparisons are planned or not, total volume of comparisons matters. Whenever conducting multiple contrasts ($c > 3$), familywise Type I error inflation corrections must be applied.

Keppel & Wickens (2004) Framework:

Argues planned comparisons motivated by formal prior empirical hypothesis do not require alpha penalties because prior grounding minimizes false discovery probability; adjustments should remain reserved for unexpected post hoc queries.

Reality:

Type I error rates compound purely as a function of the number of tests conducted, regardless of whether comparisons were planned in advance. While theoretical grounding increases the prior odds that an effect is real, it does not alter nominal alpha or sampling variance. In practice, researchers must weigh the risk of false positives against power loss, making preregistration of specific hypotheses and standard error adjustments the default standard for multiple planned tests.

Multiple t Tests Formula Utilizing $MS_{Error}$ ($MS_{Within}$)

The mathematical foundation of these comparison techniques replaces individual sample variances ($s^2_{\text{group } i}$) with the pooled error variance estimate ($\sigma^2_e$, known as $MS_{Error}$ or $MS_{Within}$) from the omnibus ANOVA model.

Standard Independent t Test
$$t = \frac{M_1 - M_2}{\sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{n_2}}}$$

Relies solely on individual sample variances ($s_1^2, s_2^2$) from the two groups being tested, discarding information from the remaining sample groups.

Omnibus Error Replacement t Test
$$t = \frac{M_i - M_j}{\sqrt{\frac{MS_{Error}}{n_i} + \frac{MS_{Error}}{n_j}}}$$

Replaces isolated variances with $MS_{Error}$ ($MS_{Within}$) across all three conditions ($MS_{Error} = 37$, $df_{Error} = n - k = 24$), yielding substantially higher statistical power.

ANOVA Assumptions & Decision Protocol

✓
Condition 1 (Homoscedasticity): All populations have the same variance ($\sigma^2_1 = \sigma^2_2 = \dots = \sigma^2_k = \sigma^2_e$), tested via Levene's test (we do not want Levene's test to be significant).
✓
Condition 2 (Equal Group Sizes): Equal sample sizes exist across each group ($n_1 = n_2 = n_3 = 9$, total $n = 27$).

Violation Protocol (When Homogeneity of Variance is Violated):

• For the omnibus test: Use Welch's ANOVA when homogeneity of variance is violated.

• For pairwise $t$ tests with equal $n_i$: Use individual group variances ($s_i^2$) and evaluate $t$ on $2(n_i - 1)$ degrees of freedom.

• For pairwise $t$ tests with unequal $n_i$: Use individual group variances with the Welch Satterthwaite degrees of freedom adjustment.

Formal APA Results Report & Guidelines

Below is the comprehensive APA format narrative summarizing the omnibus ANOVA findings and post hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni adjustment:

The omnibus ANOVA indicated that there was a significant difference in accuracy on a sustained attention task across caffeine conditions, \( F(2, 24) = 6.16 \), \( p = .007 \), \( \eta^2 = .339 \). Follow up pairwise comparisons using independent samples \( t \) tests with a Bonferroni adjusted alpha criterion of \( \alpha = .017 \) indicated that mean accuracy for high caffeine intake (\( M = 16.67 \), \( SD = 6.32 \)) was significantly higher than no caffeine intake (\( M = 6.67 \), \( SD = 6.32 \)), \( p < .017 \). However, low caffeine intake (\( M = 10.67 \), \( SD = 5.57 \)) did not significantly differ from either no caffeine intake or high caffeine intake.

Bonferroni Adjustment Protocol

When conducting all \( c = 3 \) pairwise comparisons among the three treatment groups, uncorrected tests inflate the familywise Type I error rate up to \( \alpha_{FW} \le 1 - (1 - .05)^3 \approx .143 \) (over \( 14\% \)).

To maintain the total familywise error rate at \( \alpha = .05 \), the per comparison significance threshold is adjusted using the Bonferroni inequality:

\( \alpha_{adj} = \frac{\alpha_{FW}}{c} = \frac{.05}{3} \approx .017 \)

Only pairwise differences yielding \( p < .017 \) (or equivalently, adjusted \( p \) values where \( p \times 3 < .05 \)) are considered statistically significant.
