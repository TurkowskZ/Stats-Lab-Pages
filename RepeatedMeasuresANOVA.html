Tab 1 • Experimental Design Architecture

Repeated Measures (Within Subjects) Experimental Design

In a Between Groups ANOVA, each participant is tested in only one experimental condition. Because different individuals populate each group, any preexisting person to person differences in baseline ability or physiology are trapped inside the error term. In a Repeated Measures (Within Subjects) ANOVA, every single participant completes all levels of the independent variable, fundamentally transforming how we partition variance.

Advantages

Why Researchers Choose Repeated Measures

1. Economy of Design (Fewer Participants Required) Because each person provides data across all conditions, n participants generate n × a data points. In our Cerebral Blood Flow study with a = 4 time points, just n = 8 participants yield 32 total measurements. A Between Groups design would require 32 separate individuals to achieve the same 8 observations per condition.
2. Mathematical Control for Individual Differences Every participant acts as their own baseline control. Consistent individual traits (such as resting vascular tone, baseline alertness, or general skill) are quantified as SS_Participant and subtracted directly out of the residual error variance, substantially increasing statistical power to detect true treatment effects.
3. Ability to Examine Learning & Temporal Change When the scientific question centers on change over time, such as motor learning across blocks, memory decay, or physiological fatigue across 10, 20, 30, and 40 minutes, repeated measures directly models within person trajectories.
Disadvantages

Design Vulnerabilities & Methodological Controls

1. Practice, Fatigue, and Order Effects Repeated testing can cause participants to improve simply due to familiarity with the apparatus (practice effect) or deteriorate due to exhaustion and boredom (fatigue effect).
Methodological Remedy: Researchers control practice and order effects through randomization and counterbalancing (such as complete counterbalancing or Latin Square designs) so that every experimental condition appears equally often at each ordinal position.
2. Differential Carryover Effects Differential carryover occurs when participating in one specific condition order has a different effect than participating in another order (an order by task interaction). For example, if Condition A teaches a cognitive heuristic that permanently changes how participants perform Condition B, whereas experiencing Condition B first does not alter Condition A, counterbalancing alone cannot eliminate the bias.
Methodological Remedy: Use extended washout periods between sessions or revert to a Between Groups design if one condition permanently contaminates subsequent conditions.
Crucial Clarification

Resolving the Terminology Trap: Why “SS Between” Means “SS Participant”

Same Variances, Different Source

Where Do Explained & Unexplained Variances Come From?

The exact same conceptual types of variances (variance explained by the experimental manipulation versus unexplained error variance) are computed in both Between Groups and Within Subjects designs. The critical difference is where those variances come from:

  • In Between Groups Designs: Explained variance (SS_Treatment) and unexplained variance (SS_Error) come from separate branches of SS_Total. Unexplained variance includes all individual participant differences!
  • In Within Subjects Designs: The experimental manipulation happens inside the same participants. Therefore, both explained variance (SS_Treatment) and unexplained variance (SS_Error) come from the SS_Within branch, after partitioning out variance due to individual participant differences!

How to Read “SS_Between” Without Confusion

In lecture diagrams of Within Subjects ANOVA, the left branch is labeled SS_Between. This phrasing can be confusing because in a Between Groups ANOVA, “SS Between” meant the treatment effect.

Mental Shortcut from Lecture Notes: Whenever you see SS_Between in a repeated measures diagram, mentally translate it to SS_Participant (or SS_P in factorial notation). It is simply the variability between different participants (individual differences across the 8 participants’ average scores) that we measure and remove from the error term!

Tab 2 • Parametric Assumptions in Repeated Measures

Statistical Assumptions & The Additional Assumption of Sphericity

In a One Way or Factorial Within Subjects ANOVA, the standard statistical assumptions remain the same as those noted in previous analyses: independent errors, normally distributed errors, and homoscedastic errors. However, the univariate approach for analyzing within subjects designs requires an additional assumption: Sphericity.

Conceptual Meaning

Equal Pairwise Relationships

Conceptually, sphericity assumes that the level of dependence or relationship between every pair of experimental conditions (such as Condition A with B, A with C, and B with C) is homogeneous across the levels of the within subjects factor.

Mathematical Definition

Homogeneity of Variance of Differences

Homogeneity of variance of the difference scores across the levels of the within subjects factor. If you subtract each participant’s scores for every pair of conditions and compute the variance of each difference column, sphericity holds when: Variance(A − B) ≈ Variance(A − C) ≈ Variance(B − C)

Crucial 2 Level Exemption

Why 2 Levels Never Need Mauchly’s Test

When a within subjects factor has only 2 levels (a = 2, such as Hemisphere: Left vs Right), you can compute only one column of difference scores (Left minus Right). Because there is only one variance of differences and no second variance to compare it against, the sphericity assumption is no longer a concern!

Epsilon & P Value Exercise Key

Mauchly’s Test, Univariate df Adjustments & Worked Epsilon Examples

When Mauchly’s test is statistically significant (p < .05), sphericity is violated and the unadjusted F test suffers from Type I error inflation. We can use multivariate analyses, multilevel modeling, or adjust both df_num and df_error by a fraction (ε, ranging from 1 / (a − 1) to 1).

1. Huynh Feldt (Most Powerful)

Uses the largest epsilon estimate, providing the most statistical power among the df-adjusted tests. Recommended when p < .05 and ε_GG > 0.75 (mild violation).

2. Greenhouse Geisser (Intermediate / Conservative)

Uses a lower epsilon than Huynh Feldt, making it more conservative. Recommended when p < .05 and ε_GG < 0.75 (severe violation).

3. Lower Bound (Most Conservative)

Assumes the worst possible sphericity violation by setting ε_LB = 1 / (a − 1) (for a = 4, ε_LB = 1/3 = 0.3333), reducing df_num to 1.

Repeated Measures Data Formatting: Wide Format vs. Long Format

Before running a Repeated Measures ANOVA in statistical software (such as R, SPSS, or Excel), you must structure your dataset appropriately. Unlike Between Groups designs where every row is an independent person, a repeated measures dataset contains multiple observations per participant and can be arranged in either Wide Format or Long Format.

1. Wide Format (One Row Per Participant)

8 Rows × 5 Columns

In Wide Format, each participant occupies a single row, and each level of the within subjects factor has its own separate column. This is the classic layout used for hand calculations, Excel spreadsheets, and traditional SPSS Repeated Measures menus.

Participant Time_10m Time_20m Time_30m Time_40m

2. Long Format (One Row Per Observation)

32 Rows × 3 Columns

In Long Format, each measurement occasion is stacked vertically on its own row. A participant ID column repeats across rows, an index column identifies the within subjects condition (Time), and a single column holds all DV scores (Velocity). This format is required by modern R packages (such as ezANOVA, afex, and multilevel models).

Tab 4 • Complete Hand Calculation Walkthrough (Cerebral Blood Flow)

One Way Repeated Measures ANOVA: Cerebral Blood Flow Velocity

Experimental Scenario: Cerebral blood flow velocity (cm/s) is recorded across a = 4 time points (10 mins, 20 mins, 30 mins, 40 mins) during a sustained vigilance task in n = 8 participants, yielding n × a = 32 total observations and a Grand Mean of GM = 89.0625 cm/s.

Step by Step Hand Calculation Derivations (Explicit Summation Notation)

Step 1 • Total Sum of Squares (SS_Total) df_Total = (n × a) − 1 = (8 × 4) − 1 = 31

Subtract the Grand Mean (GM = 89.0625) from each of the 32 raw scores, square each deviation, and sum across all 32 cells:

SS_Total = Σ (X − GM)²
SS_Total = (110 − 89.0625)² + (93 − 89.0625)² + … + (73 − 89.0625)² + (68 − 89.0625)²
SS_Total = 438.3789 + 15.5039 + … + 258.0039 + 443.6289 = 8401.875
Step 2 • Treatment Sum of Squares (SS_Time) df_Time = a − 1 = 4 − 1 = 3

Subtract the Grand Mean (GM = 89.0625) from each of the 4 Time treatment means (M_Time), square each deviation, sum them, and multiply by n = 8 participants per condition:

SS_Time = Σ n_i (M_Time − GM)²
SS_Time = 8 × [ (113.375 − 89.0625)² + (90.875 − 89.0625)² + (80.250 − 89.0625)² + (71.750 − 89.0625)² ]
SS_Time = 8 × [ 591.0977 + 3.2852 + 77.6602 + 299.7227 ] = 8 × 971.765625 = 7774.125
Step 3 • Participant Sum of Squares (SS_Participant) df_Participant = n − 1 = 8 − 1 = 7

Subtract the Grand Mean (GM = 89.0625) from each of the 8 participant marginal means (M_Participant), square each deviation, sum across all 8 participants, and multiply by a = 4 treatments per participant:

SS_Participant = Σ a_i (M_Participant − GM)²
SS_Participant = 4 × [ (91.25 − 89.0625)² + (92.25 − 89.0625)² + (86.75 − 89.0625)² + (87.75 − 89.0625)² + (95.00 − 89.0625)² + (85.50 − 89.0625)² + (84.75 − 89.0625)² + (89.25 − 89.0625)² ]
SS_Participant = 4 × [ 4.7852 + 10.1602 + 5.3477 + 1.7227 + 35.2539 + 12.6914 + 18.5977 + 0.0352 ] = 4 × 88.59375 = 354.375
Step 4 • Error Sum of Squares (SS_Error = Participant × Time Interaction) df_Error = (a − 1) × (n − 1) = 3 × 7 = 21

Subtract both SS_Time and SS_Participant from SS_Total to isolate the unexplained interaction between Participant and Time:

SS_Error = SS_Total − (SS_Time + SS_Participant)
SS_Error = 8401.875 − (7774.125 + 354.375) = 273.375
Step 5 • Mean Squares, Omnibus F Ratio, F Critical & Partial η² Decision: Reject Null Hypothesis (p < .0001)
MS_Time = SS_Time / df_Time = 7774.125 / 3 = 2591.3750
MS_Error = SS_Error / df_Error = 273.375 / 21 = 13.017857
F(3, 21) = MS_Time / MS_Error = 2591.3750 / 13.017857 = 199.0631 (F_critical at α = .05 is 3.0725)
η_p² = SS_Time / (SS_Time + SS_Error) = 7774.125 / (7774.125 + 273.375) = 7774.125 / 8047.500 = 0.9660 (96.60%)

Why Decompose the Interaction?

Interpreting the Significant Time × Hemisphere Interaction

Because the Time × Hemisphere interaction is significant (F(3, 21) = 11.249, p = .000131), interpreting the overall marginal main effects alone is incomplete and potentially misleading. For instance, the main effect of Hemisphere says Left (89.06) is higher than Right (79.94), but looking at the graph reveals that at 10 minutes, the two hemispheres are virtually identical (113.38 vs 113.63 cm/s)!

Two Valid Simple Effects Decomposition Pathways:
Pathway 1 (Compare Hemispheres at Each Time): 4 paired samples t tests at 10m, 20m, 30m, and 40m with Bonferroni α = .05 / 4 = .0125.
Pathway 2 (Effect of Time Within Each Hemisphere): 2 separate One Way RM ANOVAs (Left across Time; Right across Time) with Bonferroni α = .05 / 2 = .025.
