Bayes' Theorem: P(A|B) =
(P(B|A) * P(A)) (P(B|A) * P(A) + P(B|Not A) * P(Not A))

1. Three Ways to Understand Bayes

The Mathematical Formula

Bayes Theorem formalizes conditional probability to determine the probability of a hypothesis given observed data, defined as is shown at the top of this page.

For parameter estimation in psychological research, the denominator represents the marginal likelihood. We expand this using the Law of Total Probability to integrate over the entire parameter space. This calculation evaluates the probability of the hypothesis directly, offering a coherent alternative to frequentist null hypothesis significance testing and the inherent limitations of p values.

The Belief Updater

The theorem provides a normative framework for epistemic belief revision and computational modeling. Researchers begin with a prior probability distribution, P(A), quantifying existing domain knowledge.

Upon observing new empirical evidence B, this prior P(A) is multiplied by the likelihood function, P(B|A), representing the diagnostic strength of the data assuming the hypothesis is true.

Dividing this product by the marginal likelihood of the data, P(B), yields the posterior distribution, P(A|B). This posterior reflects the updated state of knowledge. In cognitive science, this precise variable mapping models optimal perception, learning, and decision making.

The Natural Frequency Filter

Translating abstract probabilities into natural frequencies reliably mitigates cognitive heuristics such as base rate neglect. Consider a clinical population of 1000 individuals where 10 have a specific pathology, which represents our prior base rate P(A). A diagnostic assessment correctly identifies 9 of these true cases, mapping directly to the formula's numerator P(B|A) * P(A).

However, the test also produces 99 false positives among the 990 healthy individuals, which maps to the second half of the denominator P(B|Not A) * P(Not A). The total number of positive test results, or the marginal likelihood P(B), is the sum of these frequencies (9 + 99 = 108). Therefore, the posterior probability P(A|B) is simply 9 out of 108. Cognitive psychology literature demonstrates that framing Bayesian inference via these discrete event counts aligns with human cognitive architecture.

2. "Real World" Demonstrations

🩺 Medical Screening

A disease affects 1% of the population. A test has a 90% hit rate and a 9% false alarm rate. If you test positive, what is the chance you are actually sick?

Mapping: P(A) = 0.01, P(B|A) = 0.90, P(B|Not A) = 0.09

(0.90 * 0.01) (0.90 * 0.01) + (0.09 * 0.99)

Result: 0.009 / 0.0981 = 0.0917. Only about 9%! The high number of false alarms in the large healthy population overwhelms the true positives.

📧 Spam Filtering

Your email algorithm knows that 20% of all emails are spam. It notices the word "Winner". Spam emails use this word 80% of the time, while normal emails use it 1% of the time.

Mapping: P(A) = 0.20, P(B|A) = 0.80, P(B|Not A) = 0.01

(0.80 * 0.20) (0.80 * 0.20) + (0.01 * 0.80)

Result: 0.16 / 0.168 = 0.952. The presence of the word "Winner" updates the system belief, making it highly probable (around 95%) that the message belongs in the junk folder.

⚙️ Quality Control

A factory machine produces defective parts 5% of the time. A laser scanner detects defects with 95% accuracy but flags good parts 2% of the time.

Mapping: P(A) = 0.05, P(B|A) = 0.95, P(B|Not A) = 0.02

(0.95 * 0.05) (0.95 * 0.05) + (0.02 * 0.95)

Result: 0.0475 / 0.0665 = 0.714. If the scanner flags a part, there is a 71% chance it is truly defective. The operator must decide if that threshold warrants throwing the part away.
